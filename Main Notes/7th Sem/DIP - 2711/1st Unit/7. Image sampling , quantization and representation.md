## 7. Image sampling , quantization and representation

**1. Digitization: Image Sampling and quantization**
- To convert analog signals into digital signals , sampling and quantization are applied. 
	- <u>Sampling</u>
		- This process digitizes the spatial coordinates (x,y).
		- It is done by taking discrete , equally spaced measurements creating a grid of samples
			- The quality of the image depends on the number of samples taken defining spatial resolution
		- If low number of samples are used , image artifact called checkerboard is noticed.
		
	- <u>Quantization</u>
		- This process digitizes the intensity of the image. 
		- It is done by converting continous intensity interval into finite set of intensity level.
			- The quality of quantization depends upon the number of intensity levels used , defining the intensity resolution 
		- False contouring happens when low number of gray levels are chosen , this effect produces ridge like structures in smooth areas of the image where no edge existed originally. It is very noticeable in gray levels less than or equal to 16.

**2. Image Representation**
- An image is represented as a 2D matrix or an array of number where each value is a pixel (picture element).
- An image of sixe  M x N (M = rows , N = Columns) is denoted by a function f(x,y) where 
$$ x = 0,1,2,...M-1$$
$$ y = 0,1,2,...N-1$$
- Cooridnate system used is the top left corner is always denoted as 0,0 , and x axis stretches to the bottom (rows) and y axis to the right (col)
- The gray levels(L) is denoted by $$L = 2^k$$ where k is the number of bits used to represent one pixel. For eg if one pixel is 8-bit then the gray levels for one pixel is $$2^8$$ i.e 256.

- The number of bits (b) required to store one image is $$b=M \times N \times k$$
**3. Spatial and Intensity Resolution**
- <u>Spatial Resolution</u> 
	- Spatial resolution is the smallest amount of discernable detail in an image.
		- It is denoted as <b>dots per inches (dpi) or line pairs per unit distance</b>
		- Higher spatial resolution means higher number of details can be picked up.
		- Very low spatial resolution leads to checkerboard image artifact.
- <u>Intensity Resolution</u>
	- Intensity resolution is the smallest discernable change in intensity.
	- It is denoted as the number of bits used to represent each pixel.
	- Higher intensity resolution (more gray levels per pixels) means more subtle shades and low risk of false contouring
- <u>Relationship between these (<b>Isopreference Curve</b>)</u>
	- For image with large number of details. For eg a crowd-scene , percieved quality depends more on the spatial resolution whereas for images with large , smooth curves , the quality depends on the number of bits used to represent each pixel to avoid image artifacts such as false contouring.

**4. Image Interpolation**
- Interpolation is the process of estimating unknown points using known data. In DIP , it is used to resize , panning , and to correct geometric distortions.
	- <u>Nearest neighbor interpolation</u>
		- Simplest form of interpolation that assigns the closest pixels intensity from the original image to the new location.
		- Pros-> Simple and fast
		- Cons -> Tends to produce blocky , jagged artifcats (Checkerboard Effect)
	- <u>Bilinear Interpolation</u> 
		- This interpolation technique uses the weighted sum of 4 nearest neighbors.
			- Pros: Offers a significant improvement over nearest neighbor
			- Cons: Can slightly blur the image
	- <u>Bicubic Interpolation</u>
		- This interpolation uses 16 nearest neighbors to estimate the pixels value. 
			- Pros: Preservers finer details than bilinear and is the standard in many imaging applications
			- Cons: Computationally Expensive.
